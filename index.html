How to revise chemistry

The quickest way to remember chemistry is to test yourself on it. Pulling an answer out of your memory makes it stick far better than reading it again, and the harder it is to remember, the stronger it sticks once you get it. Use this same routine every session.

Getting started

  1. Set a timer for 30 minutes.
  2. Open this week's topic on the Fundamental Chemistry website.
  3. Close your notes and Knowledge Organiser, then answer the first Warm-up question.

One 30-minute session

5 min
Warm up from memory. Answer three questions with your notes closed: one from this week's topic, one from last week and one from last month. Then mark them. If you got one wrong, that's your brain telling you it needs that one again.
10 min
Learn one chunk. Read one Learn box or one section of the Knowledge Organiser. Close it, then add the key points and diagrams to your summary sheet in your own words.
5 min
Make flashcards. Put each definition on a card, with the question on the front and the exact answer on the back.
10 min
Test yourself. Do the Quick Check, Fill the Blanks and one Exam-Style question with your notes closed. Mark it in a different colour, then add the mark scheme's words to your summary sheet.

Make it stick

  1. Spread it out. Three 30-minute sessions on different days work much better than one long session.
  2. Look, cover, write, check. Always cover the answer before you write. Work through a small chunk at a time, such as one row of a table or a few flashcards. Use each card daily until you get it right three or four days running, then test it again a week or two later. PMT has ready-made flashcards for every topic.
  3. Summary sheets. One double-sided A4 sheet per topic. Read it aloud three times, then write it from memory three times, filling any gaps in a different colour.
  4. Brain dump. Once a week, pick an earlier topic and give yourself 3 minutes to write down everything you can remember about it. Then check it against your summary sheet or the Recap and Connect step on the website.
  5. Mix it up later on. From February, the exam practice weeks mix questions from different topics. It feels harder, and that is why it works.

In the exam

State, give, name
A short fact.
Describe
Say what happens or what you see.
Explain
Say why, using "because" or "so".
Compare
Write about both things.
Calculate
Show every step for method marks.
Evaluate
Points for and against, then a judgement with a reason.

Use the marks as a guide: a 3-mark question needs three separate points.

The six required practicals

Questions about the required practicals make up at least 15% of the marks across Combined Science. Learn the method, the variables and how to make the results more accurate.

PracticalPaperWhat they ask

Maths you will need

Calculations come up on both papers, and the same methods are used every year.

CalculationHow
Relative formula mass (Mr)Add up the relative atomic masses of every atom. Ca(OH)2 = 40 + (16 + 1) × 2 = 74
Conservation of massMass of reactants = mass of products. 10 g CaCO3 makes 5.6 g CaO, so CO2 = 4.4 g
Concentrationmass (g) ÷ volume (dm³). Change cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. Units g/dm³
Rf valuedistance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent. No units, always less than 1
Mean ratequantity of product formed ÷ time taken. Units such as g/s or cm³/s
Relative atomic mass from isotopes(mass × % of each isotope, added together) ÷ 100
Moles HIGHER TIERmoles = mass ÷ Mr
Bond energies HIGHER TIERenergy to break bonds minus energy released making bonds. A negative answer means exothermic
Gradient of a tangent HIGHER TIERDraw a tangent to the curve, then change in y ÷ change in x gives the rate at that time
